\begin{abstract}
Neural network training via back-propagation requires global gradient propagation across all layers, which imposes memory overhead, limits parallelism, and hinders biological plausibility. The recently proposed NoProp (No Propagation) framework circumvents these issues by decomposing the network into independently trained blocks, each learning to denoise a noisy target through a local diffusion process. While computationally attractive, NoProp lacks an explicit mechanism to incorporate prior physical knowledge about the system generating the data. \rev{In this paper, we propose a physics-informed NoProp framework that integrates a governing equation discovered by SPIDER (Sparse Physics-Informed Discovery of Empirical Relations) into blockwise learning. The framework combines the diffusion-based local learning paradigm of NoProp with weak-form sparse regression: the validated equation coefficients enter the model through a learned first-frame physical condition and local temporal-field constraints. We evaluate the framework using 15 independent }\revmath{64^3}\rev{ trajectories of unforced decaying homogeneous isotropic turbulence, with trajectory-disjoint discovery, validation, and test splits. SPIDER recovers the complete momentum equation with a maximum coefficient error of }\revmath{0.368\%}\rev{ and an independent test residual of }\revmath{9.70\times10^{-4}}\rev{. In three-seed experiments, physics-informed NoProp achieves }\revmath{86.73\%}\rev{ and }\revmath{82.94\%}\rev{ mean accuracy in the low- and high-enstrophy groups, compared with }\revmath{17.59\%}\rev{ and }\revmath{17.46\%}\rev{ for the architecture-matched no-equation NoProp control, while reducing the corresponding full Navier--Stokes residuals by }\revmath{24.8\%}\rev{ and }\revmath{63.5\%}\rev{.}
% physics-informed NoProp not only achieves competitive accuracy in downstream tasks but also significantly improves robustness to noise (up to

\section{Introduction}

Deep neural networks have achieved remarkable successes across a wide range of scientific and industrial domains, largely driven by the effectiveness of back-propagation (BP) for training multi-layer architectures \cite{rumelhart1986learning}. BP iteratively adjusts network parameters by propagating an error signal from the output back through all layers, enabling end-to-end optimization of complex models. Despite its empirical success, BP suffers from several fundamental limitations. First, it is biologically implausible, as it requires symmetric forward and backward passes that do not align with local synaptic plasticity observed in the brain \cite{grossberg1987competitive,lillicrap2016random}. Second, BP necessitates storing intermediate activations during the forward pass, imposing significant memory overhead that hampers scalability \cite{rumelhart1986learning}. Third, the sequential nature of gradient propagation creates strong dependencies across layers, limiting parallelization and impeding efficient distributed training \cite{carreira2014distributed}. These drawbacks have motivated a sustained interest in developing alternative learning paradigms that do not rely on global error propagation \cite{hinton2006fast,lee2015difference,nokland2016direct,jaderberg2017decoupled,hinton2022forward}.

Among these alternatives, \textbf{NoProp} (No Propagation) \cite{li2025noprop} has recently emerged as a promising framework that completely avoids forward and backward propagation across network blocks. Inspired by diffusion models \cite{sohl2015deep,ho2020denoising,song2021maximum}, NoProp decomposes a neural network into independently trained blocks. During training, each block receives the input $x$ and a noisy version of the target label embedding, and learns to denoise it using only local back-propagation within the block. At inference, Gaussian noise is progressively transformed through the blocks into a clean representation that is fed to a final classifier. By eliminating global gradient flow, NoProp enables blockwise parallel training, reduces memory footprint, and offers greater biological plausibility. Empirical results on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-100 demonstrate that NoProp achieves performance competitive with standard back-propagation on the same architectures \cite{li2025noprop}.

However, NoProp, like most purely data-driven deep learning methods, does not explicitly incorporate any prior knowledge about the physical processes that generated the data. In many scientific applications -- such as fluid dynamics, climate modeling, or materials science -- the observed data are governed by fundamental physical laws expressed as partial differential equations (PDEs). Ignoring this structure can lead to models that, while accurate on training data, produce predictions that violate physical constraints, especially under distribution shift or in the presence of noise \cite{raissi2019physics}. This limitation is particularly acute when data are sparse or corrupted, a common scenario in experimental measurements.

Concurrently, a separate line of research has focused on \textbf{data-driven discovery of governing equations} from observational data. Starting with sparse regression methods like SINDy \cite{brunton2016discovering} for ordinary differential equations, numerous approaches have been developed to identify PDEs from spatiotemporal data \cite{rudy2017data,schaeffer2017learning,raissi2018hidden,gurevich2019robust,reinbold2020using,messenger2021weak,kaheman2020sindy}. A particularly robust framework is \textbf{SPIDER} (Sparse Physics-Informed Discovery of Empirical Relations) \cite{gurevich2024learning}, which combines three key ingredients: (i) physical constraints of smoothness, locality, and symmetry to construct compact libraries of candidate terms; (ii) a weak formulation of PDEs that shifts derivatives onto test functions, drastically reducing sensitivity to noise; and (iii) sparse regression based on singular value decomposition to extract parsimonious relations. SPIDER has been successfully applied to highly turbulent channel flow data, recovering not only the Navier--Stokes and continuity equations but also the pressure-Poisson equation, energy equation, and boundary conditions, even under extreme noise levels \cite{gurevich2024learning}.

Despite the power of SPIDER for equation discovery, it remains a post-hoc analysis tool that does not directly inform the training of neural networks used for downstream tasks such as prediction or classification. Conversely, NoProp offers an efficient learning engine but lacks a mechanism to enforce physical consistency. Bridging these two paradigms could yield models that are both computationally efficient and physically faithful, a goal that aligns with the emerging field of \textbf{physics-informed machine learning}\cite{raissi2019physics,karniadakis2021physics,willard2020integrating,karpatne2017theory}.

\rev{In this paper, we propose \textbf{physics-informed NoProp}, a framework that integrates a governing equation discovered by SPIDER into the blockwise training of NoProp. Our approach proceeds in two stages. First, we apply SPIDER to trajectory-disjoint observational data to recover and validate the complete incompressible momentum equation in four-dimensional weak form, yielding explicit and interpretable coefficients. Second, we transfer these coefficients into NoProp through two complementary paths. In the conditioning path, the coefficients combine first-frame convective, pressure, and viscous energy-rate terms into a target-free physical condition that is encoded and fused into every NoProp block. In the local-constraint path, a pretrained and frozen temporal decoder reconstructs velocity and pressure time windows from each block latent $z_t$, and the weak momentum and continuity residuals augment that block's local denoising objective without propagating gradients between blocks. The pressure--Poisson and kinetic-energy relations are algebraically derived from the discovered momentum equation and are evaluated only in the corresponding ablation variants.}

\rev{We validate the proposed method on an independently generated dataset of unforced decaying homogeneous isotropic turbulence, comprising 15 trajectory-disjoint direct numerical simulations on }$64^3$\rev{ periodic grids.} \rev{Our experiments demonstrate that physics-informed NoProp substantially improves future energy-decay classification over the architecture-matched no-equation NoProp control while producing latent reconstructions with lower Navier--Stokes residuals.} \rev{Under controlled observation noise, the model remains predictive at a noise level of 0.1 channel standard deviations, although accuracy decreases in both enstrophy groups and approaches chance at higher noise levels.} \rev{Ablation studies further evaluate the effects of the discovered momentum equation and the algebraically derived pressure--Poisson and kinetic-energy relations on predictive accuracy and physical consistency.}

The main contributions of this work are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce a principled framework that combines the efficiency of NoProp's blockwise diffusion-based learning with the interpretability of SPIDER's data-driven equation discovery.
    \item \rev{We design a two-path physics interface that uses the discovered equation coefficients both in a learned first-frame physical condition and in a strictly local weak-form loss evaluated on frozen temporal-decoder reconstructions, without propagating gradients between NoProp blocks.}
    \item We provide extensive empirical evidence on a challenging turbulent flow dataset, showing that the proposed method yields physically consistent representations, characterising its behaviour under controlled noise, and achieving strong predictive performance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This section reviews the literature most germane to the proposed physics-informed NoProp framework. We first discuss alternative learning paradigms that avoid global back-propagation, with a focus on the NoProp algorithm. Next, we survey data-driven methods for discovering partial differential equations (PDEs) from observations, emphasizing the SPIDER methodology. We then briefly cover physics-informed machine learning and diffusion-based generative models, which provide the theoretical underpinnings for our approach. Finally, we position our contribution in relation to these lines of work.

\subsection{Local Learning and Alternatives to Back-Propagation}
\label{sec:local-learning}

The canonical training algorithm for deep neural networks, back-propagation (BP) \cite{rumelhart1986learning}, relies on a global error signal that is propagated backwards through all layers. While highly effective, BP has several well-documented shortcomings: it is biologically implausible because it requires symmetric forward and backward passes \cite{grossberg1987competitive,lillicrap2016random}, it incurs a large memory footprint due to the need to store intermediate activations \cite{rumelhart1986learning}, and its sequential nature hinders parallelization \cite{carreira2014distributed}.

To overcome these limitations, a variety of \textbf{local learning} schemes have been proposed. Early examples include deep belief networks trained layer-wise with contrastive divergence \cite{hinton2006fast}. More recent methods include feedback alignment \cite{lillicrap2016random} and direct feedback alignment \cite{nokland2016direct}, which replace the symmetric weights of BP with fixed random matrices, yet still require a global error signal. Synthetic gradients \cite{jaderberg2017decoupled} train auxiliary networks to approximate the gradient at each layer, allowing some decoupling. The forward-forward algorithm \cite{hinton2022forward} trains each layer by contrasting the responses to positive and negative examples using a local objective.

Among these, \textbf{NoProp} (No Propagation) \cite{li2025noprop} stands out as a method that completely eliminates both forward and backward propagation across blocks. NoProp treats a neural network as a sequence of $T$ blocks, each performing a denoising step inspired by diffusion models \cite{sohl2015deep,ho2020denoising,song2021maximum}. Formally, let $x$ be an input and $y$ its label, with associated embedding $u_y \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (either fixed one-hot or learnable). NoProp defines a forward (inference) process:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:noprop-forward}
z_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I),\qquad 
z_t = a_t\,\hat{u}_{\theta_t}(z_{t-1},x) + b_t z_{t-1} + \sqrt{c_t}\,\epsilon_t,\quad \epsilon_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),
\end{equation}
for $t=1,\dots,T$, where $\hat{u}_{\theta_t}$ is a neural network block and $a_t,b_t,c_t$ are scalars derived from a noise schedule. The training objective for block $t$ is derived from a variational lower bound on the conditional log-likelihood $\log p(y|x)$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:noprop-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NoProp}}^{(t)} = \frac{T}{2}\,\eta\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathrm{SNR}(t)-\mathrm{SNR}(t-1))\|\hat{u}_{\theta_t}(z_{t-1},x)-u_y\|^2\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{SNR}(t)=\bar\alpha_t/(1-\bar\alpha_t)$ is the signal-to-noise ratio, $\eta$ a hyperparameter, and the expectation is taken over the variational posterior. A final classifier $\hat{p}_{\theta_{\text{out}}}(y|z_T)$ is trained with cross-entropy. Crucially, each block can be updated independently using only local back-propagation, enabling massive parallelism and reducing memory consumption.

Despite its computational advantages, NoProp does not incorporate any prior knowledge about the physical laws that may govern the data. In scientific applications, such knowledge can be critical for generalization and robustness.

\subsection{Data-Driven Discovery of Governing Equations}
\label{sec:equation-discovery}

A parallel line of research aims to extract symbolic equations -- particularly PDEs -- directly from observational data. The \textbf{sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics} (SINDy) framework \cite{brunton2016discovering} pioneered this approach for ordinary differential equations by posing a sparse linear regression problem over a library of candidate functions. For PDEs, several extensions have been developed \cite{rudy2017data,schaeffer2017learning,raissi2018hidden,gurevich2019robust,reinbold2020using,messenger2021weak,kaheman2020sindy}, but they often suffer from high sensitivity to noise because they operate on the \textbf{strong form} of the equations, which requires numerical differentiation of noisy data.

To mitigate noise sensitivity, \textbf{weak-form} formulations have been introduced \cite{gurevich2019robust,reinbold2020using,messenger2021weak}. The key idea is to multiply the PDE by a smooth test function $w$ and integrate over a spatio-temporal domain $\Omega$, shifting derivatives onto $w$ via integration by parts. For a candidate relation of the form $\sum_n c_n f_n = 0$, this yields a linear system:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weak-form}
\sum_n c_n \int_\Omega w\,f_n \,d\Omega = 0,
\end{equation}
where the integrals can be approximated numerically without ever differentiating the data. This weak formulation dramatically improves robustness, enabling accurate identification even with noise levels exceeding $100\%$ \cite{gurevich2024learning}.

The \textbf{SPIDER} (Sparse Physics-Informed Discovery of Empirical Relations) framework \cite{gurevich2024learning} synthesises these ideas into a comprehensive methodology. SPIDER incorporates three key ingredients:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Symmetry-constrained library construction.} Libraries of candidate terms are built by considering irreducible representations of the symmetry group of the system (e.g., $O(3)$ for three-dimensional isotropic fluids). This yields compact libraries that respect rotational and reflectional symmetries, reducing the search space and improving interpretability. For example, for a fluid described by velocity $\mathbf{u}$ and pressure $p$, the scalar library up to third order is:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:scalar-library}
    \mathcal{L}_0^{(3)} = \begin{aligned}[t]
    \{&1,\,p,\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u},\,\partial_t p,\,p^2,\,u^2,\,p^3,\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla p,\\
    &\nabla^2 p,\,p\partial_t p,\,\partial_t^2 p,\,p\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u},\,u^2 p,\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\partial_t\mathbf{u}\},
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    and the vector library is:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:vector-library}
    \mathcal{L}_1^{(3)} = \begin{aligned}[t]
    \{&\mathbf{u},\,\partial_t\mathbf{u},\,\nabla p,\,p\mathbf{u},\,(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u},\,\nabla^2\mathbf{u},\\
    &\partial_t^2\mathbf{u},\,u^2\mathbf{u},\,p^2\mathbf{u},\,\partial_t\nabla p,\,p\nabla p,\\
    &\mathbf{u}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}),\,(\nabla\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u},\,\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}),\,p\partial_t\mathbf{u},\,u\partial_t p\}.
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Weak formulation and feature matrix construction.} For each term $f_n$ in the library, its weak-form integral over many domains $\Omega_i$ (with different test functions $w_i$) is computed, forming a feature matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ with entries
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:feature-matrix}
    Q_{in} = \frac{1}{V_i S_n}\int_{\Omega_i} w_i(\mathbf{x},t)\,f_n(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\Omega,
    \end{equation}
    where $V_i$ is a normalisation factor and $S_n$ is a scale that non-dimensionalises the term.
    
    \item \textbf{Sparse regression via singular value decomposition.} Relations of the form $\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{f}=0$ correspond to vectors $\mathbf{c}$ in the nullspace of $\mathbf{Q}$. SPIDER identifies parsimonious relations by examining the right singular vectors associated with the smallest singular values of $\mathbf{Q}$. A greedy algorithm then prunes terms to produce a sequence of increasingly sparse candidate equations, from which the most appropriate is selected based on a residual threshold.
\end{enumerate}

\rev{In the cited SPIDER study, the method was applied to the Johns Hopkins turbulence database and recovered incompressible-flow relations under noisy observations \cite{li2008public,gurevich2024learning}. The present work instead reruns the discovery protocol on the independently generated HIT DNS described in Section~\ref{sec:dataset}.}

\subsection{Physics-Informed Machine Learning}
\label{sec:piml}

The integration of physical knowledge into neural network training has gained substantial traction under the banner of \textbf{physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)} \cite{raissi2019physics,karniadakis2021physics}. PINNs embed the residual of a known PDE into the loss function, enabling the network to approximate solutions while respecting the governing physics. For a PDE $\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{u}]=0$, the loss is typically
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pinn-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PINN}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + \lambda \|\mathcal{R}[\hat{\mathbf{u}}]\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{u}}$ is the network output. This paradigm has been extended to inverse problems, parameter estimation, and discovery of unknown terms \cite{willard2020integrating,karpatne2017theory}. However, PINNs assume the form of the PDE is known \textit{a priori} -- an assumption that limits their applicability in purely data-driven discovery scenarios.

\subsection{Diffusion Models and Flow Matching}
\label{sec:diffusion}

NoProp is deeply rooted in the theory of \textbf{denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs)} \cite{sohl2015deep,ho2020denoising,song2021maximum}. In DDPMs, a forward process gradually adds noise to data, and a neural network learns to reverse it. The training objective is a weighted sum of denoising score matching losses. The continuous-time limit leads to score-based generative models described by stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and probability flow ODEs \cite{song2021maximum}. \textbf{Flow matching} \cite{lipman2022flow,tong2023improving} provides an alternative simulation-free training objective for continuous normalising flows, directly regressing a vector field that transports a prior to the data distribution. Both diffusion and flow matching have been adapted for supervised learning tasks such as classification and regression \cite{han2022card,kim2025simulation,hu2024flow}, demonstrating their versatility.

NoProp adapts these ideas to a discriminative setting by treating the label embedding as the target distribution. The forward (noising) process is fixed, and each block learns a conditional denoising step. The resulting framework is highly modular and parallelisable.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}
\label{sec:positioning}

The preceding discussion highlights a clear gap: NoProp provides an efficient local learning mechanism but lacks physical awareness, while SPIDER excels at discovering interpretable PDEs from data but is a post-hoc tool. The emerging field of physics-informed machine learning has shown the benefits of embedding physical constraints into neural networks, yet it typically assumes the governing equations are known. \rev{\textbf{Physics-informed NoProp} bridges these worlds: it uses SPIDER to discover and validate the complete momentum equation directly from the available data, then incorporates its coefficients both as a predictive first-frame condition and as a weak-form local regulariser in the NoProp training objective. This synergy yields a model that preserves blockwise locality while improving predictive and physical consistency.} To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to combine blockwise diffusion-based learning with automated equation discovery in a unified framework.

\label{sec:methodology}

\rev{We present the proposed physics-informed NoProp framework, which transfers a SPIDER-discovered and independently validated momentum equation into the blockwise training of NoProp. Section~\ref{sec:noprop-recall} briefly recalls the NoProp algorithm \cite{li2025noprop} and establishes notation. \subsection{NoProp: Blockwise Diffusion-Based Learning}
\label{sec:noprop-recall}

Let $x\in\mathcal{X}$ be an input (e.g., an image) and $y\in\{1,\dots,m\}$ its associated label. An embedding $u_y\in\mathbb{R}^d$ of the label is defined, either as a fixed one-hot vector or as a row of a learnable embedding matrix $W_{\text{Embed}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}$. NoProp \cite{li2025noprop} decomposes a neural network into $T$ sequential blocks. During \textbf{inference}, a latent variable $z_0$ is drawn from a standard Gaussian prior $p(z_0)=\mathcal{N}(z_0;0,I)$. For each block $t=1,\dots,T$, the latent is updated by a residual diffusion step:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:inference-step}
z_t = a_t\,\hat{u}_{\theta_t}(z_{t-1},x) + b_t z_{t-1} + \sqrt{c_t}\,\epsilon_t,\quad \epsilon_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),
\end{equation}
where $\hat{u}_{\theta_t}:\mathbb{R}^d\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^d$ is a neural network parameterised by $\theta_t$, and $a_t,b_t,c_t$ are scalar coefficients determined by a fixed noise schedule (e.g., cosine schedule). The final latent $z_T$ is fed into a classifier $\hat{p}_{\theta_{\text{out}}}(y|z_T)$, typically a linear layer followed by softmax.

\textbf{Training objective.} NoProp is trained by maximising a variational lower bound on the conditional log-likelihood $\log p(y|x)$. A forward noising process $q(z_t|y)$ is defined as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process that gradually adds noise to the label embedding $u_y$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:noising-process}
q(z_t|y) = \mathcal{N}\bigl(z_t;\,\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,u_y,\;1-\bar\alpha_t\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=t}^T\alpha_s$ and $\alpha_s$ are related to the noise schedule. Using the reparameterisation trick, one can sample $z_{t-1}\sim q(z_{t-1}|y)$ directly. The evidence lower bound (ELBO) simplifies to a sum of per-block denoising score matching losses \cite{li2025noprop,song2021maximum}. For block $t$, the loss is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diffusion-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}}^{(t)} = \frac{T}{2}\,\eta\,\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{t-1}|y)}\Bigl[ \bigl(\mathrm{SNR}(t)-\mathrm{SNR}(t-1)\bigr)\,\bigl\|\hat{u}_{\theta_t}(z_{t-1},x)-u_y\bigr\|^2 \Bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{SNR}(t)=\bar\alpha_t/(1-\bar\alpha_t)$ is the signal-to-noise ratio and $\eta$ is a hyperparameter. The classifier is trained with cross-entropy:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:classification-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}} = -\mathbb{E}_{q(z_T|y)}\bigl[\log\hat{p}_{\theta_{\text{out}}}(y|z_T)\bigr].
\end{equation}
The total NoProp loss (per sample) is $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}} + \sum_{t=1}^T\mathcal{L}_{\text{diff}}^{(t)}$ plus an often-neglected KL term $D_{\text{KL}}(q(z_0|y)\|p(z_0))$ \cite{li2025noprop}.

\subsection{SPIDER: Discovering PDEs from Data}
\label{sec:spider-summary}

SPIDER \cite{gurevich2024learning} is a data-driven method for discovering interpretable PDEs from possibly noisy observations of spatiotemporal fields. Consider a physical system described by fields $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)$ (e.g., velocity) and $p(\mathbf{x},t)$ (pressure). The method assumes that the governing equations can be written as linear combinations of candidate terms $f_n$ -- products of the fields and their partial derivatives -- respecting the symmetries of the system. The goal is to find a sparse coefficient vector $\mathbf{c}=[c_1,\dots,c_N]^\top$ such that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pde-candidate}
\sum_{n=1}^N c_n f_n(\mathbf{u},p,\partial_t\mathbf{u},\nabla\mathbf{u},\nabla p,\dots)=0.
\end{equation}

To avoid numerical differentiation of noisy data, SPIDER employs a \textbf{weak formulation}. Let $\Omega_i$ be a spatiotemporal domain and $w_i(\mathbf{x},t)$ a smooth test function with compact support. Integrating \eqref{eq:pde-candidate} against $w_i$ and integrating by parts where possible yields:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weak-formulation}
\sum_{n=1}^N c_n \int_{\Omega_i} w_i(\mathbf{x},t)\,\tilde{f}_n(\mathbf{u},p,\dots)\,d\Omega = 0,
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{f}_n$ denotes the term after transferring derivatives onto $w_i$ (if integration by parts is applicable) or the original term otherwise. Repeating this for many domains $i=1,\dots,M$ and different test functions gives a linear system $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}$, with $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$ having entries
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:feature-matrix-spider}
Q_{in} = \frac{1}{V_i S_n}\int_{\Omega_i} w_i(\mathbf{x},t)\,\tilde{f}_n(\mathbf{u},p,\dots)\,d\Omega,
\end{equation}
where $V_i=\int_{\Omega_i}|w_i|\,d\Omega$ is a normalisation factor and $S_n$ is a scale that non-dimensionalises term $n$ (estimated from characteristic scales of the data). The system is solved for $\mathbf{c}$ with $\|\mathbf{c}\|=1$ by taking the right singular vector corresponding to the smallest singular value of $\mathbf{Q}$.

Sparse, parsimonious relations are obtained via a greedy algorithm that iteratively removes terms and tracks the residual $\|\mathbf{Q}^{(N)}\mathbf{c}^{(N)}\|$. The final selection balances accuracy and sparsity using a threshold on the relative increase in residual. \rev{The original SPIDER publication demonstrated this procedure on the Johns Hopkins database; here, the accepted artifact is independently discovered and validated on the generated HIT trajectories (Section~\ref{sec:discovered-equations}).}

\subsection{Physics-Informed NoProp}
\label{sec:physics-noprop}

\rev{Physics-informed NoProp couples equation discovery to local learning through a validated, machine-readable interface. The pipeline has three stages: (i) generate independent, densely sampled turbulence trajectories and identify a sparse four-dimensional relation; (ii) validate its support and coefficients on unseen trajectories and persist the complete provenance; and (iii) use the accepted relation both as a first-frame physical condition and in a weak-form temporal-field loss inside each isolated NoProp objective. An artifact that fails the discovery, support, coefficient, or held-out-residual checks is not admitted to training.}

\subsubsection{Discovering Governing Equations with SPIDER}
\label{sec:discovering-equations}

\rev{For the unforced incompressible flow considered here, the target momentum relation is}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:navier-stokes}
\revmath{\displaystyle \partial_t\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}+\nabla p-\nu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}=0.}
\end{equation}
\rev{The vector candidate library contains the four terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:navier-stokes} and the same-order distractor $\nabla|\mathbf{u}|^2$. For a compactly supported time--space test function $w$, integration by parts gives the componentwise weak columns}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weak-residuals}
\revmath{\displaystyle
\begin{aligned}
q_i^{(t)}&=-\int u_i\,\partial_t w\,d\Omega, &
q_i^{(c)}&=-\int u_i u_j\,\partial_jw\,d\Omega,\\
q_i^{(p)}&=-\int p\,\partial_iw\,d\Omega, &
q_i^{(\nu)}&=\int u_i\,\nabla^2w\,d\Omega.
\end{aligned}}
\end{equation}
\rev{Thus neither temporal nor spatial derivatives are taken directly from the observed fields. Nine trajectories are used for discovery, three additional trajectories for validation, and three remain untouched until final testing. One hundred bootstrap refits resample complete trajectories rather than individual weak rows. SPIDER selects}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:discovered-momentum}
\revmath{\displaystyle
\partial_t\mathbf u+1.003459(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
+1.003470\nabla p-0.00501841\nabla^2\mathbf u=0.}
\end{equation}
\rev{The DNS viscosity is $0.005$ and is consulted only after discovery. The maximum coefficient error is $0.368\%$; validation and test residuals are $9.51\times10^{-4}$ and $9.70\times10^{-4}$, respectively. The next-best support has residual $3.86\times10^{-2}$, and all 100 trajectory-bootstrap refits select the same four terms. SPIDER discovers the momentum equation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:discovered-momentum}; incompressibility,}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:continuity}
\revmath{\displaystyle \nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0,}
\end{equation}
\rev{is evaluated separately as a physical constraint. The pressure--Poisson and kinetic-energy relations used in later ablations are algebraically derived from the accepted momentum equation rather than independently discovered.}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\rev{Equation-Conditioned First-Frame Representation}}{Equation-Conditioned First-Frame Representation}}
\label{sec:equation-condition}

\rev{The downstream label is a short-horizon kinetic-energy-decay quantile, so the first-frame energy balance supplies information that a purely spatial image feature can discard. For the first observed frame, let $K=\frac12\langle|\mathbf u|^2\rangle$ and define}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:energy-rate-condition}
\revmath{\displaystyle
e_m(x)=\frac{\langle\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf q_m(x)\rangle}{K},\qquad
s_{\mathbf c}(x)=c_2e_c(x)+c_3e_p(x)+c_4e_\nu(x),}
\end{equation}
\rev{where $\mathbf q_c=(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u$, $\mathbf q_p=\nabla p$, $\mathbf q_\nu=\nabla^2\mathbf u$, and $(c_2,c_3,c_4)$ are loaded from the accepted equation. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:navier-stokes}, $s_{\mathbf c}$ is the instantaneous relative kinetic-energy decay rate. Its derivatives are computed from the first frame only; neither future frames nor class labels enter Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy-rate-condition}.}

\rev{A three-layer strided 3-D convolutional encoder preserves local spatial structure before pooling. Its feature is fused with a two-layer embedding of the discovery-set-standardised scalar $s_{\mathbf c}$. The spatial encoder, physics encoder, and fusion layer are jointly optimised during supervised condition pretraining and then frozen before local block training. The no-equation ablation disables the coefficient-derived scalar, the analytic ablation uses the exact Navier--Stokes coefficients, and discovered physics-informed NoProp uses the coefficients in Eq.~\eqref{eq:discovered-momentum}.}

\subsubsection{Reconstructing Physical Fields from Latent Variables}
\label{sec:reconstructing-fields}

\rev{Full momentum residuals require a temporal field rather than a single reconstructed snapshot. We therefore use the shared decoder}
\begin{equation}
\revmath{\displaystyle
\mathcal D_\phi:\mathbb R^{128}\rightarrow
\mathbb R^{9\times4\times16\times16\times16},}
\end{equation}
\rev{which jointly reconstructs nine velocity--pressure frames. The decoder is pretrained against trajectory windows and then frozen. Stored discovery-set means and standard deviations return its outputs to physical units before weak integration, keeping the coefficients in Eq.~\eqref{eq:discovered-momentum} in the units in which SPIDER measured them.}

\subsubsection{Weak-Form Physics Loss for Each Block}
\label{sec:weak-physics-loss}

\rev{For block $t$, test function $k$, and velocity component $i$, let $q_{t,k,i}^{(m)}$ denote the four weak columns in Eq.~\eqref{eq:weak-residuals} evaluated on the decoded time window. We define the coefficient-normalised momentum residual}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:normalised-ns-residual}
\revmath{\displaystyle
r_{t,k,i}^{\mathrm{NS}}=\sum_{m=1}^{4}c_mq_{t,k,i}^{(m)},\qquad
\eta_{t,k,i}^{\mathrm{NS}}=
\frac{r_{t,k,i}^{\mathrm{NS}}}
{\sum_m|c_mq_{t,k,i}^{(m)}|+\epsilon}.}
\end{equation}
\rev{The coefficients $c_m$ are loaded from the validated artifact. Incompressibility is evaluated separately using $r^{\mathrm{div}}=-\int\tilde{\mathbf u}\cdot\nabla w\,d\Omega$ and the analogous contribution-wise normalisation $\eta^{\mathrm{div}}$. The main local physical loss is}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:physics-loss}
\revmath{\displaystyle
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{phys}}^{(t)}=
\operatorname{mean}_{k,i}|\eta_{t,k,i}^{\mathrm{NS}}|^2
+0.25\operatorname{mean}_{k,\tau}|\eta_{t,k,\tau}^{\mathrm{div}}|^2.}
\end{equation}
\rev{The relation ablations add the algebraically derived pressure--Poisson and kinetic-energy residuals only in their corresponding variants. They are not treated as additional SPIDER discoveries.}

\subsubsection{Overall Training Objective and Algorithm}
\label{sec:overall-objective}

\rev{The conceptual sum of the isolated block objectives is}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total-loss}
\revmath{\displaystyle
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{local}}=\sum_{t=1}^T
\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}^{(t)}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{phys}}^{(t)}\right).}
\end{equation}
\rev{We implement the cosine schedule in the generative direction. The increasing signal fractions and the local training marginal satisfy}
\begin{equation*}
\revmath{\displaystyle
0=\gamma_0<\cdots<\gamma_T=1,\qquad
z_t=\sqrt{\gamma_t}u_y+\sqrt{1-\gamma_t}\epsilon.}
\end{equation*}
\rev{Block $t$ predicts $u_y$ with a uniformly weighted local squared error. At inference, deterministic coefficients are chosen as}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:consistent-schedule}
\revmath{\displaystyle
b_t=\sqrt{\frac{1-\gamma_{t+1}}{1-\gamma_t}},\qquad
a_t=\sqrt{\gamma_{t+1}}-b_t\sqrt{\gamma_t},\qquad c_t=0.}
\end{equation}
\rev{With a perfect local prediction, this update has exactly the training marginal at $\gamma_{t+1}$; hence the first block sees pure noise in both training and inference and the final block reaches a clean target embedding.}

\rev{At each local update we sample $J\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{1,\ldots,T\}$ and optimise $T(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}^{(J)}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{phys}}^{(J)})$, an unbiased estimator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:total-loss}. Block $J$ has its own optimiser and all other blocks receive no gradient. The spatial encoder, physical-rate encoder, fusion layer, label embedding, and decoder are pretrained once and frozen. After local block training, the classifier is trained on $z_T$ detached from every block, so its loss cannot propagate into the local learners.}

The training procedure is summarised in Algorithm~\ref{alg:training}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\color{black}
\caption{Training of Physics-Informed NoProp}
\label{alg:training}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE \rev{Training data, $T$ blocks, validated SPIDER artifact, pretrained encoder and fusion modules, label embedding, and frozen decoder $\mathcal D_\phi$.}
\FOR{\rev{each local update}}
    \STATE \rev{Sample mini-batch $\mathcal B$ and block $J\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{1,\ldots,T\}$.}
    \STATE \rev{Compute detached condition $E(x,s_{\mathbf c}(x))$ and target embedding $u_y$.}
    \STATE \rev{Sample the input latent from block $J$'s matching noise-to-signal marginal.}
    \STATE \rev{Predict locally with $\theta_J$ and decode its output using frozen $\mathcal D_\phi$.}
    \STATE \rev{Load the discovered coefficients and compute Eq.~\eqref{eq:physics-loss}.}
    \STATE \rev{Update only $\theta_J$ using $T(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{diff}}^{(J)}+\lambda\mathcal L_{\mathrm{phys}}^{(J)})$.}
\ENDFOR
\STATE \rev{Freeze all blocks and train $\theta_{\mathrm{out}}$ on detached final latents $z_T$.}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:implementation}

\rev{\textbf{Choice of test functions.} Following \cite{gurevich2019robust,gurevich2024learning}, we use time--space tensor-product test functions:}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:test-function}
\revmath{\displaystyle
w(\mathbf{x},t)=\bigl(1-\tilde t^2\bigr)^\beta
\prod_{\xi\in\{x,y,z\}}\bigl(1-\tilde\xi^2\bigr)^\beta.}
\end{equation}
\rev{Every local coordinate is mapped to $[-1,1]$. Discovery and training use $\beta=4$ with deterministic cosine modulations. The temporal derivative, spatial gradient, and Laplacian weights required by the discovered momentum relation are evaluated analytically.}

\rev{\textbf{Integration domains.} Discovery samples two nine-frame windows and four $16^3$ spatial domains per trajectory. Eight test functions and all three momentum components yield 192 weak rows per trajectory. Training applies the same eight-function bank to each decoded nine-frame $16^3$ field.}

\rev{\textbf{Derivative computation.} Equation~\eqref{eq:weak-residuals} moves every derivative in the discovered momentum relation onto $w$. Consequently, SPIDER discovery and the main momentum--continuity loss use field products and contractions with fixed test-function weights, without differentiating the DNS observations or decoder outputs. The optional pressure--Poisson ablation constructs a fixed test-function Hessian once on the grid, while the optional kinetic-energy ablation evaluates its dissipation term with differentiable grid gradients of the decoded velocity.}

\rev{\textbf{Decoder and condition architecture.} A compact transposed-convolutional decoder maps $z_t\in\mathbb R^{128}$ to $9\times4\times16^3$ values and is frozen after pretraining. The spatial condition encoder uses three $3\times3\times3$ convolutions with channel widths 16, 32, and 64, followed by $2^3$ adaptive pooling and a 128-dimensional projection. A two-layer 32-unit multilayer perceptron embeds Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy-rate-condition}, and a linear fusion layer returns the 128-dimensional condition.}

\rev{\textbf{Training.} We use $T=10$ independently optimised blocks, AdamW with learning rate $10^{-3}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch size 32, mixed precision, and TF32. Each block receives 100 local updates with $\lambda=10^{-2}$; the classifier is then trained for 30 epochs on detached final latents. Matched comparisons use seeds $\{42,123,456\}$ and the same architecture, data splits, and optimisation protocol.}

\textbf{Extension to continuous time.} NoProp also admits a continuous-time variant (NoProp-CT) \cite{li2025noprop} where the number of blocks tends to infinity and the evolution of $z_t$ is described by a stochastic differential equation. In that setting, the physics-informed loss can be defined analogously by sampling times $t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)$ and evaluating the weak residuals at those times. The diffusion loss becomes an integral over time involving $\mathrm{SNR}'(t)$. For brevity, we focus on the discrete-time formulation; the continuous-time extension is straightforward.

\subsubsection{Baseline Methods}
\label{sec:baselines}

\rev{We organise the comparison into architecture-matched physics ablations and broader learning baselines. The three matched local learners are:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \rev{\textbf{No-equation NoProp:} the complete PI-NoProp architecture with the coefficient-derived condition disabled and }\revmath{\lambda=0}\rev{. This is the primary structure-matched control.}
    \item \rev{\textbf{Analytic-NS NoProp:} the exact coefficients }\revmath{(1,1,1,-0.005)}\rev{ are used in both the first-frame condition and temporal weak loss.}
    \item \rev{\textbf{PI-NoProp (discovered NS):} the validated SPIDER coefficients are loaded from the discovery artifact into the same two paths.}
\end{itemize}

\rev{We additionally evaluate five broader baselines:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \rev{\textbf{Reference NoProp (3-D):} a one-hot NoProp adaptation of \cite{li2025noprop} in which every block owns an independent 3-D field encoder, denoiser, and optimiser. It has no supervised shared pretraining or physical input.}
    \item \rev{\textbf{NoProp-CT (3-D conditional flow):} a continuous-time adaptation that learns the velocity of a Gaussian-to-one-hot conditional interpolation by simulation-free flow matching and uses ten-step RK4 integration at inference.}
    \item \rev{\textbf{CNN (BP):} a supervised 3-D convolutional classifier trained on the first frame by global back-propagation.}
    \item \rev{\textbf{Global physics-informed BP:} an encoder--classifier using the same discovered first-frame rate and temporal decoder as PI-NoProp, trained end-to-end with classification, reconstruction, and weak-physics losses. This is the implemented PINN-style global-gradient comparison \cite{raissi2019physics}.}
    \item \rev{\textbf{SPIDER rate + classifier:} multinomial logistic regression fitted to the single discovered first-frame rate in Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy-rate-condition}. This deliberately strong interpretable baseline uses no future information.}
\end{itemize}

\rev{All eight methods use the same trajectory-disjoint samples and seeds }\revmath{\{42,123,456\}}\rev{, but they are not falsely presented as parameter matched. Their fitted parameter counts are approximately 3.71M for each matched PI-NoProp variant, 3.04M for reference NoProp, 0.313M for NoProp-CT, 0.137M for CNN (BP), 1.41M for global physics-informed BP, and 10 for the scalar SPIDER classifier.}

\subsubsection{Implementation Details for PI-NoProp}
\label{sec:implementation-details}

\rev{\textbf{Network architecture.} The three matched PI-NoProp variants use identical 128-dimensional condition and latent spaces and }\revmath{T=10}\rev{ independently optimised denoising blocks. A three-layer 3-D convolutional encoder processes the first }\revmath{4\times16^3}\rev{ field, a two-layer multilayer perceptron embeds the discovered scalar rate, and a linear layer fuses both features. The shared transposed-convolutional decoder maps a block latent to }\revmath{9\times4\times16^3}\rev{ velocity--pressure values. Low- and high-enstrophy groups use the same architecture.}

\rev{\textbf{Training details.} All neural models use AdamW with learning rate }\revmath{10^{-3}}\rev{, weight decay }\revmath{10^{-4}}\rev{, and batch size 32. For the matched variants, the condition modules are pretrained for at most 30 epochs with validation early stopping, the temporal decoder for 40 epochs, and the label embeddings are aligned to discovery-set class centroids. These shared components are then frozen. Each NoProp block receives 100 local updates with }\revmath{\eta=0.1}\rev{ and }\revmath{\lambda=10^{-2}}\rev{ for the physical variants; the classifier is trained for 30 epochs on detached final latents. The weak loss uses }\revmath{\beta=4}\rev{, eight fixed test functions, and nine-frame }\revmath{16^3}\rev{ domains.}

\rev{Reference NoProp likewise receives 100 updates for each of ten independent one-hot blocks but has no shared pretraining. NoProp-CT receives 1000 conditional-flow updates and uses fixed-step ten-step RK4 inference. CNN (BP) is trained for 40 epochs; global physics-informed BP is trained for at most 30 epochs with patience eight using }\revmath{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CE}}+0.05\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rec}}+0.01\mathcal L_{\mathrm{phys}}}\rev{. The SPIDER scalar baseline uses standardisation followed by multinomial logistic regression with }\revmath{C=100}\rev{.}

\textbf{Evaluation metrics.} We report:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \rev{\textbf{Classification accuracy:} trajectory-disjoint top-1 test accuracy, reported as mean and sample standard deviation over three training seeds.}
    \item \rev{\textbf{Physical consistency:} for every decoded weak row, the discovered-coefficient momentum residual is normalised contribution-wise:}
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:relative-residual}
    \revmath{\displaystyle
    \eta_{k,i}^{\mathrm{NS}}=
    \frac{\sum_{m=1}^{4}c_mq_{k,i}^{(m)}}
    {\sum_{m=1}^{4}|c_mq_{k,i}^{(m)}|+\epsilon},\qquad
    \eta_{\mathrm{NS}}=
    \sqrt{\operatorname{mean}_{k,i}|\eta_{k,i}^{\mathrm{NS}}|^2}.}
    \end{equation}
    \rev{The reported value is batch-averaged over test samples; }\revmath{\eta_{\mathrm{div}}}\rev{ is formed analogously from the three weak divergence contributions. Relation ablations additionally report }\revmath{\eta_{\mathrm{PP}}}\rev{ and }\revmath{\eta_{\mathrm{energy}}}\rev{. A dash is used when a baseline does not reconstruct a temporal field.}
    \item \rev{\textbf{Noise robustness:} clean-trained predictive accuracy and }\revmath{\eta_{\mathrm{NS}}}\rev{ under the seven-level observation-noise protocol, and SPIDER support, coefficient error, bootstrap support, and held-out residual under the separate nine-level discovery-noise protocol.}
    \item \rev{\textbf{Computational efficiency:} measured wall-clock training time, fitted parameter count, and peak allocated GPU memory. Timings are labelled by training phase rather than treated as identical epoch budgets across unlike methods.}
\end{itemize}

\rev{Figure~\ref{fig:training-convergence} reports optimisation diagnostics from the six current PI-NoProp runs without additional training or test-set tuning. Over 100 updates per block, the diffusion term decreases by nearly two orders of magnitude and the total local objective by more than one order of magnitude, while the normalised physics term remains bounded near its initial scale. After the local blocks are frozen, both low- and high-enstrophy validation accuracies stabilise near 90\% within the first few classifier epochs.}

\subsection{Robustness to Noise}
\label{sec:noise-robustness}

\rev{We separate corruption of a clean-trained predictor from corruption of the fields used for equation discovery. These protocols have different noise grids and failure scales and are therefore not combined into a single robustness claim.}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\rev{Clean-Trained Prediction under Observation Noise}}{Clean-Trained Prediction under Observation Noise}}

\rev{The PI-NoProp and architecture-matched no-equation checkpoints are trained only on clean inputs. At test time, Gaussian noise is added to the standardised first frame and the first-frame physical terms are recomputed from that same corrupted observation. Five independent observation-noise realisations are averaged for each of three model seeds, while the inference latent is fixed across noise levels and repetitions so that the varied randomness is the observation noise alone. Figure~\ref{fig:prediction-noise} reports both enstrophy groups.}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../paper/figures/fig_noise.png}
\caption{\rev{Clean-trained prediction under additive observation noise. (a) Five-class test accuracy; lines are three-seed means after averaging five noise realisations per seed, shading denotes one sample standard deviation across model seeds, and the dotted line marks chance performance. (b) Contribution-normalised momentum residual of the decoded fields under the same protocol. Background bands distinguish the empirically stable, degrading, and near-chance regimes; they are descriptive regions rather than additional fitted thresholds.}}
\label{fig:prediction-noise}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \rev{At }\revmath{\sigma\leq0.05}\rev{, PI-NoProp remains close to its clean accuracy. At 5\% channel-standard-deviation noise, the low- and high-enstrophy accuracies are 85.06\% and 81.75\%, decreases of only 1.67 and 1.19 percentage points from the clean results.}
    \item \rev{Degradation becomes substantial beyond this range: accuracy is 80.19\%/74.68\% at }\revmath{\sigma=0.1}\rev{ and 60.86\%/61.03\% at }\revmath{\sigma=0.2}\rev{ for the low-/high-enstrophy groups. At }\revmath{\sigma=0.5}\rev{ it falls to 24.69\%/30.56\%, and at }\revmath{\sigma=1.0}\rev{ to 17.90\%/21.03\%, which is at or near five-class chance. The no-equation model is already near chance under clean input and remains so throughout the sweep.}
    \item \rev{The decoded-field residual does not track predictive utility monotonically. For PI-NoProp, }\revmath{\eta_{\mathrm{NS}}}\rev{ changes from 0.432 to 0.377 in the low-enstrophy group and from 0.274 to 0.198 in the high-enstrophy group as }\revmath{\sigma}\rev{ increases from 0 to 1. A smoother or more class-agnostic decoded field can have a smaller weak residual even when classification has collapsed; consequently, the high-noise residual is not interpreted as evidence of predictive robustness.}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\rev{SPIDER Discovery under DNS-Field Noise}}{SPIDER Discovery under DNS-Field Noise}}

\rev{The second protocol adds Gaussian noise to the physical DNS velocity and pressure fields before weak-feature construction and reruns support selection and independent validation at each level. To follow the transition into failure over a wide range, this sweep uses permissive tracking gates: full four-term NS support, validation and test residuals no larger than 0.25, maximum coefficient error no larger than 200\%, bootstrap support of at least 50\%, and support separation of at least 1.25. These gates are used only for the corruption sweep and do not replace the stricter clean-discovery criteria in Section~\ref{sec:discovered-equations}. Figure~\ref{fig:spider-discovery-noise} reports the resulting coefficient, support, and residual diagnostics.}

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\rev{The experimental results show that integrating the SPIDER-discovered momentum equation and its derived physical relations into NoProp's blockwise training yields a coherent combination of predictive, physical, and computational benefits:}

\rev{The central contribution is the PI-NoProp interface rather than equation discovery alone. The discovered coefficients enter the model through two coordinated paths: a learned first-frame physical condition is fused into every NoProp block, and a frozen temporal decoder converts each block latent into a nine-frame field window whose weak momentum and continuity residuals are evaluated within that block's local objective. The first path supplies target-free physical information before denoising, whereas the second path shapes the latent representation after each local update. Because the shared encoders and decoder are frozen during blockwise optimisation, this coupling preserves NoProp's locality while giving the blocks a direct physical training signal.}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Improved accuracy:} \rev{The physics-informed condition and local residual provide a strong inductive bias, raising five-class accuracy from the near-chance no-equation control to 86.73\% and 82.94\% in the low- and high-enstrophy groups.}
    \item \textbf{Physics-aware robustness:} \rev{The weak formulation avoids differentiating noisy observations, supports validated SPIDER discovery through the low-noise regime, and preserves strong clean-trained prediction through 10\% channel-standard-deviation observation noise.}
    \item \textbf{Physically meaningful representations:} \rev{The local physics loss lowers the measured momentum residual and produces substantially larger latent class separation in both enstrophy groups, as confirmed by centroid distances and silhouette scores.}
    \item \textbf{Local and memory-efficient optimisation:} \rev{PI-NoProp retains strictly block-local updates and uses less than 0.31~GB peak memory in the measured protocol, while the added temporal decoder and weak-form evaluations provide an explicit and interpretable computational cost.}
\end{enumerate}

Limitations include the dependence on the quality of SPIDER-discovered equations (which may themselves contain small errors due to discretisation) and the additional hyperparameter $\lambda$ that requires tuning. Future work could explore adaptive weighting schemes or end-to-end discovery of both the equations and the network parameters.



\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented \textbf{physics-informed NoProp}, a novel framework that integrates data-discovered partial differential equations into the blockwise diffusion-based learning paradigm of NoProp. By combining the computational efficiency and biological plausibility of local learning with the interpretability and physical fidelity of SPIDER-discovered governing equations, our approach addresses a fundamental limitation of purely data-driven neural network training in scientific applications: the lack of explicit mechanisms to enforce consistency with the underlying physical laws that generated the observations.

\rev{The proposed method proceeds in two stages. First, SPIDER discovers and validates the sparse four-term momentum relation on the generated HIT DNS; continuity is imposed separately, and pressure--Poisson and energy relations are retained only as artifact-derived ablations. Second, the accepted momentum coefficients enter PI-NoProp through two paths: a learned first-frame physical condition is fused into every NoProp block, and a frozen temporal decoder maps each block latent to a field window whose \textbf{weak-form physics residual} is evaluated in that block's local objective. For a given block $t$ with latent variable $z_t$, the decoder $\mathcal{D}_\phi$ reconstructs the physical fields $(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_t,\tilde{p}_t)$.}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:physics-loss-conclusion}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{phys}}^{(t)} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{K}|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}} \Bigl( \bigl\| \mathcal{R}_{\text{NS}}^{(w_k)}(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_t,\tilde{p}_t) \bigr\|^2 + \bigl\| \mathcal{R}_{\text{cont}}^{(w_k)}(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_t) \bigr\|^2 \Bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}_{\text{NS}}^{(w_k)}$ and $\mathcal{R}_{\text{cont}}^{(w_k)}$ denote the weak-form residuals of the Navier--Stokes and continuity equations against test functions $w_k$. \rev{This loss is fully differentiable and, for the momentum and continuity terms, avoids numerical differentiation of the observed DNS fields. In the implementation, the NoProp blocks are first optimised independently with local diffusion and physics objectives, after which the blocks are frozen and a classifier is trained on detached final latents. The separate objectives are:}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:block-loss-conclusion}
\revmath{\displaystyle \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{block}}^{(t)}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}^{(t)}+\lambda_t\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{phys}}^{(t)},\qquad t=1,\ldots,T.}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:classifier-loss-conclusion}
\revmath{\displaystyle \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}=-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log\hat{p}_{\theta_{\mathrm{out}}}(y\mid\operatorname{stopgrad}(z_T))\right].}
\end{equation}

\rev{We validated the framework on 15 independently generated decaying homogeneous-isotropic-turbulence DNS trajectories in a periodic }\revmath{64^3}\rev{ box at }\revmath{\nu=0.005}\rev{ (Section~\ref{sec:dataset}). Extensive experiments yielded the following key findings:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Improved predictive performance.} \rev{PI-NoProp reaches 86.73\% and 82.94\% on the low- and high-enstrophy groups, respectively. It exceeds the architecture-matched no-equation control by 69.14 and 65.48 percentage points, while the specialised ten-parameter SPIDER rate classifier remains slightly higher (Section~\ref{sec:main-results}).}
    
    \item \textbf{Noise behaviour.} \rev{Under clean-trained prediction, 10\% channel-standard-deviation noise yields 80.19\% accuracy in the low-enstrophy group and 74.68\% in the high-enstrophy group; both groups approach five-class chance by 50--100\%. SPIDER retains its complete support through 0.5\% DNS-field noise under the stated corruption protocol; higher levels expose support and coefficient failures (Section~\ref{sec:noise-robustness}).}
    
    \item \textbf{Physical consistency.} \rev{Relative to the no-equation control, PI-NoProp lowers the low-enstrophy NS residual from 0.574 to 0.432 and the continuity residual from 0.293 to 0.139. These residual comparisons support improved consistency, but they do not establish that every physical metric is uniformly lower across all seeds or regions (Section~\ref{sec:main-results}).}
    
    \item \textbf{Interpretable latent space.} \rev{PCA and t-SNE projections, together with 128-D centroid and silhouette statistics, show improved separation of the five decay classes for the physics-informed variants. Within-class spread is not uniformly reduced, so the result is interpreted as a separation effect rather than proof of a smooth ordered manifold (Section~\ref{sec:representation-analysis}).}
    
    \item \textbf{Computational efficiency.} \rev{PI-NoProp preserves block-local optimisation and uses less than 0.31~GB peak memory in the measured protocol, but its physics evaluations make it slower than the lightweight CNN and global weak-form baseline. The efficiency result is therefore a locality and memory result, not a universal wall-clock speed advantage (Section~\ref{sec:efficiency}).}
\end{itemize}

\rev{Ablation studies (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}) show that increasing $\lambda$ over the tested range improves the NS residual without a clear accuracy penalty, while additional pressure--Poisson and energy relations mainly improve their corresponding consistency metrics. The decoder comparison gives the convolutional decoder a lower NS residual at similar accuracy, but the parameter counts are not matched and the linear decoder has lower continuity residual; therefore, the result is an informative architecture baseline rather than evidence of uniform architectural superiority.}

\textbf{Limitations.} Several limitations of the current work should be acknowledged. First, the quality of the physics constraint depends on the accuracy of the SPIDER-discovered momentum equation and the numerical quality of the generated DNS; the pressure--Poisson and energy relations are derived extensions whose reliability should be assessed for each dataset. Second, the hyperparameter $\lambda_t$ balancing the physics and diffusion losses requires tuning; our sweep covers $0$ through $0.1$, while broader ranges and adaptive weighting schemes remain possible future improvements. Third, the current framework assumes that the discovered relation is valid globally across the analysed HIT domain. In systems with strong spatial heterogeneity (e.g., multiphase flows or materials with interfaces), region-specific or parametric equations may be necessary.

\textbf{Future work.} The synergy between local diffusion-based learning and automated equation discovery opens several promising research directions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{End-to-end discovery and learning.} Rather than discovering equations with SPIDER in a separate pre-processing step, one could integrate the sparse regression directly into the training loop, enabling simultaneous optimisation of the network parameters and the discovery of the governing PDEs. This would allow the equations to adapt to the representations learned by the network, potentially uncovering new physics.
    
    \item \textbf{Adaptive physics weighting.} Develop meta-learning or Bayesian approaches to automatically tune the physics loss weights $\lambda_t$ based on validation performance or uncertainty estimates, reducing the need for manual cross-validation.
    
    \item \textbf{Extension to other physical systems.} Apply physics-informed NoProp to a broader range of scientific domains, such as active matter \rev{\cite{marchetti2013hydrodynamics,ramaswamy2010active}}, plasma physics \rev{\cite{levko2024datadriven}}, climate modelling, and biomedical fluid dynamics, where first-principles models are often incomplete or unknown.
    
    \item \textbf{Handling parametric and heterogeneous systems.} Generalise the framework to systems with spatially or temporally varying coefficients by representing them as linear combinations of basis functions \rev{\cite{rudy2019data}} and discovering the coefficient fields simultaneously.
    
    \item \textbf{Theoretical analysis.} Investigate the regularisation effect of the weak-form physics loss from the perspectives of statistical learning theory and dynamical systems, potentially linking it to notions of stability, generalisation, and robustness.
    
    \item \textbf{Integration with continual learning.} Since NoProp naturally supports blockwise training without global gradient flow, it is well-suited for continual learning scenarios. Incorporating physical constraints could further mitigate catastrophic forgetting by anchoring the representations to invariant physical laws \rev{\cite{delange2021continual}}.
\end{itemize}

In conclusion, physics-informed NoProp demonstrates that \textbf{local learning and global physics can coexist and reinforce each other}. By embedding the discovered momentum equation as a soft condition and local constraint in a diffusion-based blockwise architecture, we obtain a model that combines strong predictive accuracy and physical consistency with block-local optimisation and moderate memory usage. We believe this work represents a significant step toward the broader goal of \textbf{scientifically grounded deep learning} -- a paradigm where neural networks are not just predictive tools, but active participants in the discovery and representation of physical knowledge.
